Most species have multiple samples (different grain sizes, localities, purities) — this chart lists one representative BECK_AREF file each, chosen because the Beckman's 0.2–3 µm range covers the most of your target 0.25–3.5 µm span. Where no BECK measurement exists for a species, the next-best ASD file (0.35–2.5 µm) is used instead, marked ASD. Every other sample for a species sits alongside it in the same source folder, sharing its filename prefix.
Detected Bands are the five most prominent reflectance minima (absorption features) found per spectrum, computed from the splib07a ASCII reflectance data via scipy.signal.find_peaks on the smoothed, inverted curve — not read from the GIF plots. Values marked * fall in the ~0.95–1.15 µm or ~1.75–1.85 µm windows, where ASD/Beckman detector-splice joins are known to introduce artifacts (per ds1035.pdf); treat these as possible instrumental artifacts rather than confirmed mineral bands. This is an automated, unreviewed extraction — not a substitute for checking against the literature or the source plots.
Formulas are idealized end-member compositions from standard mineralogical references, not derived from ds1035.pdf or the spectral data itself (neither source contains chemistry). Species marked v are solid solutions with genuinely variable composition (element substitution across a range) — the formula shown is a representative/simplified form, not a fixed stoichiometry.

Bands Inside vs. Outside USB4000 Range

Per mineral: how many of its detected bands fall inside the USB4000's 0.20–1.10 µm window versus outside it, grouped into the four HIT classes from Abstract EANA2026-69 (Phyllosilicates, Hydrated Sulfates, Hydrated Silica, Carbonates), sorted by in-range count within each group. A count alone is not a diagnosis — identification needs a specific co-occurring pattern of bands, not just band presence — so read this as "how much raw material the USB4000 would even have to work with," not "which minerals it can identify."

44% of the 62 minerals (27 of 62) have zero detected bands inside 0.20–1.10 µm, and another 26 have exactly one — so 85% of species have at most a single band in range, nowhere near enough for pattern-based identification. Only 9 minerals clear 2 bands, mostly iron-bearing sulfates and phyllosilicates (Fe electronic transitions are genuinely visible in Vis-NIR) — whether even 2 bands is diagnostically sufficient for those species is a literature question, not something this count answers on its own.
